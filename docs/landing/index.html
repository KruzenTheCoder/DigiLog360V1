The problem

Why incidents are discovered too late

A paper book cannot raise the alarm.

The occurrence book has run security operations for a century. It has one fatal property: it only speaks when somebody opens it — and by then the night is over.

Written from memory

Entries are filled in at the end of a twelve-hour shift, long after the event, with the details worn smooth.

Nobody knows where anyone is

An officer walks into a live intrusion and the control room only finds out when they stop answering the radio.

No clock on the response

Nothing measures the gap between something happening and somebody acting on it. What isn't measured isn't managed.

Evidence you cannot produce

Weeks later a client asks what happened. The answer is a photocopy of somebody's handwriting, if the book can be found at all.

The cost is not paperwork. It is an officer standing next to a threat that nobody in the control room knows about, and a contract that cannot be defended when the client asks for proof.

The solution

One connected system, from the field to the archive

Capture it at the source. Everything else follows.

DigiLog 360 replaces the occurrence book, the patrol clock, the visitor register and the key ledger with a single platform — a phone in the field, a live console in the control room, and one database that never forgets.

The story

One incident, end to end

A perimeter breach, minute by minute.

This is the same night twice: once on DigiLog 360, and once in the book. Every timestamp below is recorded by the platform itself — nobody types these in afterwards.

Act one — it happens

FRAME 01 02:14:07 Field

Two figures cut the perimeter fence.

An officer is eleven minutes into a patrol route. A beam trips and he sees them from thirty metres — bolt cutters, no vehicle yet. He does not walk closer. He takes out his phone.

FRAME 02 02:14:47 Mobile

Forty seconds to file it — with evidence.

Type: intrusion. Severity: Critical. Two photographs and a nine-second voice note describing what he can see. There is no signal at the fence line, so it queues on the handset and files itself the moment the phone finds a bar.

The incident lands on the live board with a banner. Critical severity sets the response target — resolve within the hour, post an update every thirty minutes — and the countdown starts without anyone touching it.

Who it is for

Six roles, one version of the truth

Officer
Works the site from a phone. Clocks on, runs patrols, logs incidents, staffs the gate and hands over at the end of the shift — signing in with an employee number and a PIN.
Supervisor
Runs the shift. The site board, the team's live positions, patrol oversight, and the ability to close out a patrol somebody forgot to end.
Control room
Holds the line. Watches every open incident against its clock, logs what comes in over the radio, assigns reviewers and answers for the response.
Manager
Signs it off. Reviews every incident — acknowledge, escalate or reject with notes — and reports on how the team actually performed.
Administrator
Shapes the operation. People, sites, response targets, incident types and integrations — tuned per organisation without touching code.
Platform owner
Runs the platform. Many client organisations from one deployment, each sealed off from the others, each with its own branding, permissions and rules.
